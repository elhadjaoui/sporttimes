To organise a football match, fix four things before you invite anyone: the format, the time, the pitch and who can join. Then put the lineup where everyone can see it, so players claim a spot instead of replying "maybe" in a group chat.

The rest of this guide goes through each step, the problems that usually kill a match, and a short checklist for match day.

## Why matches fall apart in the group chat

Most casual matches don't fail on the pitch. They fail in the chat, days before. The pattern is always the same:

- Someone asks "who's in Sunday?" and gets ten replies, half of them "maybe".
- The time or the pitch changes halfway through the thread, and not everyone sees it.
- Nobody knows the real count until the evening before.
- Two people drop out on the day, and the organiser spends the morning messaging replacements.

A chat is good for talking. It's bad at keeping one clear answer to "who is playing, where and when". Everything below is about keeping that answer in one place.

## Step 1: Pick the format

The format decides how many players you need, so pick it first.

| Format | Players on the pitch | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5-a-side | 10 | Small pitches, short notice, a weekly game |
| 7-a-side | 14 | A bigger group and more space |
| 11-a-side | 22 | Full pitches and organised groups |

For a first match, 5-a-side is the easiest to fill. Most groups can find ten players, and if one drops out you can still play four against five.

Plan for one or two substitutes on top. Someone always arrives late or picks up a knock, and subs let everyone rest without stopping the game.

## Step 2: Fix the time and book the pitch

Pick the time before you ask who's free. "Sunday at 18:30" gets clear answers; "this weekend?" starts a debate.

When you book:

- Book for the format you picked. A 5-a-side pitch is too small for 14 players.
- Check what the booking includes: balls, bibs, changing rooms, lights for evening games.
- Note the cancellation rules, so you know the last moment you can call it off without paying.
- Agree how the cost is split before the match, not after.

Then send one message with all of it: date, kick-off time, pitch, format and how long you've booked. That message is the reference. If anything changes, update it and tell everyone.

## Step 3: Decide who can join

Before you invite anyone, decide whether the match is open or invite-only.

- **Open**: anyone in your group can take a spot until it's full. It's the fastest way to fill a match.
- **Invite-only**: you approve each player. Better when you want balanced teams or the pitch is small.

Either way, decide who has the final word. One organiser who approves players avoids the "I thought I was in" argument on match day.

## Step 4: Put the lineup where everyone can see it

This is the step that changes the most. Instead of counting replies, keep a lineup: one list with every spot and who has it.

A visible lineup does three things:

1. Players see how many spots are left, so they commit instead of waiting.
2. Everyone sees the same count, so nobody turns up to a match that was called off.
3. When someone drops out, the empty spot is obvious and someone else can take it.

A shared note or a spreadsheet works. What matters is that there is one list and it's always current.

## Step 5: Plan for dropouts

Someone will drop out. Plan for it before it happens:

- Keep one or two substitutes on a waiting list, and tell them they're first in line.
- Set a cut-off, such as the evening before. After it, anyone who drops out finds their own replacement.
- When a spot opens, offer it to the waiting list first, then to the whole group.

## Step 6: Pick fair teams

Uneven teams kill a match faster than rain. A few simple ways to split:

- **Captains pick**: two players take turns choosing. Quick, but the last picks remember it.
- **Mix by position**: make sure each team has a goalkeeper (or a rotation), defenders and attackers.
- **Swap at half-time**: if one team is clearly stronger, move a player across after the first half.

If the same group plays every week, rotate the captains and keep mixing the teams, so nobody is always on the losing side.

## Match-day checklist

- Lineup full, substitutes confirmed
- Final reminder sent with the time and the pitch
- Ball, bibs and a pump
- Water
- Pitch paid for
- Teams decided before kick-off, not on the pitch

## Quick answers

### How many players do I need for five-a-side?

Ten on the pitch, five per team. Add one or two substitutes for dropouts and tired legs.

### How far ahead should I organise a match?

For a weekly game, fix the time and the pitch once and keep them. For a one-off, a week ahead gives people time to commit and you time to fill the gaps.

### What if not enough people sign up?

Drop to a smaller format: from 7-a-side to 5-a-side, or four against four. A smaller game where everyone plays beats a cancelled one.

### How do I stop people dropping out at the last minute?

Set a cut-off, keep a waiting list and make the lineup visible. People drop out less when they can see the team counting on them.
